### 2026-08-19 ２回目メッセージ

Dear Dr. Kalibata,

Thank you for pushing back — that's a fair question, and I should have been more precise the first time.

By "local organic waste" I mean source-segregated agricultural and food-processing residues — livestock manure, crop residue, food-processing byproducts — not unsorted municipal waste, which is a different problem I'm not proposing to solve here. On safety: heavy metals are not eliminated but immobilized, bound into humic complexes with reduced bioavailability — that needs soil-level verification in any pilot, not just a claim. On pathogens, the process sustains 80–100°C for 24 hours, which exceeds EPA's PFRP benchmark (55°C for 3 days) by a wide margin.

But I'd rather put something in front of you that I don't have a settled answer for, because I suspect it's closer to your own training than to mine.

The example I keep coming back to wasn't something anyone set out to prove. A farmer who already used a multi-species microbial consortium in his crop operation started feeding a sugar-water solution of it to his own hives — not as a chalkbrood remedy he was shopping for, just an extension of what he already trusted for farming. Every year since, standard practice, zero chalkbrood (Ascosphaera apis) incidence. Not a controlled comparison — I can't rule out his apiary sitting in conditions less favorable to the fungus to begin with — but a livelihood on the line every season, discovered almost by accident rather than sought out, which is its own kind of scrutiny.

What puzzles me is the shape of the outcome, not just the fact of it. Single-agent biocontrol usually gives a dose-dependent, gradual reduction in infection. What's been reported here looks more like a threshold — infection or none, with little gradient in between, sustained year over year. My working hypothesis is that niche pre-emption and antibiotic secretion by different members of the consortium are acting on the pathogen's effective R0 simultaneously, and R0 crossing 1 produces exactly that kind of non-linear switch rather than a smooth decline. I know the theory; I don't have the epidemiological rigor to know if I'm reading the pattern correctly. Given your background, I'd value you tearing into that logic rather than being polite about it — and given that ~75% of crops depend on pollinators, if the mechanism is real, it matters well beyond bees.

On what I'm actually asking: I'm not looking for AGRA's endorsement. I'm trying to find out where local organic waste management is actually failing smallholders in the programs you run — contamination risk, financing, something else — before I say anything more about what we do.

Best,
Kaz Shimojo
BioNexus Holdings
