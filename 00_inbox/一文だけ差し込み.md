Dear Dr. Kalibata,

Thank you for pushing back — that's a fair question, and I should have been more precise the first time.

To be clear on what I'm asking: I'm not looking for AGRA's endorsement or a platform to pitch. I'm trying to understand where the real obstacles are in the work you're already leading, to see whether what we've built over many years of fieldwork could actually reduce one of them — not replace your strategy, but serve it.

On your specific concern: by "local organic waste" I mean source-segregated agricultural and food-processing residues — livestock manure, crop residue, food-processing byproducts — not unsorted municipal waste, which is a different and much harder problem I'm not proposing to solve here.

On safety, briefly and precisely: heavy metals are not eliminated but immobilized — bound into humic complexes with sharply reduced bioavailability, which would need soil-level verification in any pilot, not just a claim. On pathogens, the process sustains 80–100°C for 24 hours, which exceeds EPA's PFRP pathogen-reduction benchmark (55°C for 3 days) by a wide margin on both temperature and time.

One more thing, so there's no ambiguity about where I'm coming from: what interests me is not exporting a product into Africa. It's whether the processing capacity itself — the manufacturing, not just imported inputs — can be built and owned locally. That's the frame I think in, and it shapes what I'd want any collaboration to look like.

I'd rather earn a second conversation by being precise than by being persuasive.

So let me come back to my original question, because I think it matters more than anything I could tell you about our own work: in the programs you're running now, where is local organic waste management actually failing smallholders — is it the contamination risk you raised, is it financing, is it something else entirely? I'd genuinely like to know where you see the gap before I say anything more about what we do.

Best,
Kaz Shimojo
BioNexus Holdings
